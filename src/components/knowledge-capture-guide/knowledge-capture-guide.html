When important work depends on one person's memory, that person becomes a bottleneck—even when they're doing everything right. It can slow down onboarding, make it harder to take on more customers, and leave the business exposed when an experienced employee is away or moves on.

Capturing business knowledge isn't about documenting every task. It's about making the expertise your team depends on easier to share and less likely to get lost.

Where does your business rely on one person?

Think about the questions that repeatedly go to the owner or a senior employee. What work waits for them? What do new hires take a long time to learn? Where does someone say, "Only Pat knows how that works"?

Those are signs of business risk, not a criticism of the person. The goal is to make the company more resilient while respecting the judgment and experience that got it this far.

Why aren't a folder of documents and a shared drive enough?

Information is only useful if people can find it, trust that it is current, and understand when it applies. A file nobody checks—or two documents that say different things—may leave your team no better off than before.

Good guidance reflects how the work is actually done. It also makes clear when a situation calls for experience rather than a written instruction. The people who do the work should be part of deciding what is worth preserving.

What should you focus on first?

Start with one area where the dependence on a particular person is holding back the team or creating a risk the business can't ignore. Consider the effect on customer service, growth, employee onboarding, and your own time.

Success doesn't mean producing the most documentation. It means the team can handle more of the routine work with confidence, while the experienced person remains available for the situations that genuinely need their judgment.

An illustrative example: a team that relies on its manager

A service company has one manager everyone asks before moving a job forward. This works while the manager is available, but slows the team and makes growth harder. The owner doesn't need an enterprise knowledge system to see the issue: the company has become dependent on one person's experience. Making the common guidance easier to share can help the team work with more confidence and let the manager focus on the exceptions.

How do you keep business knowledge from walking out the door?

Pay attention to the work only a few people can explain, then make reducing that dependence a business priority. The right answer may be clearer guidance, better training, or a change to how the work is organized. Technology can help, but understanding what the business needs comes first.
