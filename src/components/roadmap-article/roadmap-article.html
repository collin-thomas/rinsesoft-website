How to turn a business problem into a technology roadmap

You know what your business does today. You have an idea of what you'd like to happen instead. You shouldn't have to design the technical solution before someone can help.

My starting point is simple: tell me what you do now and what you'd like to have happen, and I can help you get there. The work in between is understanding the problem, making the choices explicit, and putting the right changes in the right order.

A technology roadmap is that path: a sequence of process and systems changes tied to a business outcome. It is not a shopping list of software. And it shouldn't begin by persuading you to care about problems you didn't come to solve.

1. Describe what happens now, not which tool you want.

Start with one recurring situation. "Our team spends Friday piecing reports together" tells us more than "we need a dashboard." Walk through the last time it happened: who did the work, where the information came from, what they waited on, and where they had to check with you.

Use a real example rather than an ideal process diagram. Ask the people doing the work to show you the spreadsheet, handoff, or repeated step. If work keeps coming back to the owner, trace what was missing before it reached your desk.

2. Make the desired outcome specific.

"More efficient" is a direction, not a way to decide whether a change worked. Try: "The team can send an accurate customer update without asking me" or "We can see which jobs need attention before a customer calls."

Choose a useful measure: minutes spent preparing a report, the number of owner interruptions, or the time between a request and a reliable answer. Record today's baseline where you can. If the data isn't available, agree how to collect it rather than inventing a savings estimate.

3. Find the gap before choosing the technology.

What prevents the current workflow from producing that outcome? Missing information? Unclear ownership? Disconnected systems? An exception only one person knows how to handle? Those are different problems, even when they all look like "too much manual work."

Sometimes a clearer handoff is enough. Sometimes existing software can do more than it's doing today. Other times integration, custom software, or AI is worth considering. The choice should follow the work, the constraints, and the expected value, not the other way around.

Useful discovery can uncover a better possibility than the one you first described. Put it on the table, explain the tradeoff, and let the owner decide. A new opportunity isn't permission to quietly expand the scope.

4. Put the changes in an order that makes sense.

Pick one useful improvement, then identify what it depends on. A reporting project may need consistent job statuses before it needs a new screen. An automation may need a clear exception path before anyone should let it run unattended.

For each step, name the outcome, the owner, the dependency, the effort or uncertainty, and how you'll check it. Separate what you know from what still needs investigating. Keep later work conditional on what the first step teaches you.

A worked example: customer updates without the chase

This is an illustrative scenario, not a client case study or a claim of measured results.

Today: a customer asks for a job update. An account manager checks email and a spreadsheet, asks operations for the latest status, and escalates to the owner when the answers disagree.

Desired outcome: the account manager can give a reliable update without involving the owner, except for agreed exceptions.

  1. Agree what "current" means. Define the job statuses, who updates them, and how quickly. Review a small sample of recent requests to establish the current wait and escalation rate.
  2. Test one shared source. Use the existing system if it can hold the necessary information. Pilot with one team and make missing or stale updates visible.
  3. Connect only what the pilot justifies. If duplicate entry still creates delays, evaluate an integration. Check permissions, failure handling, and cost before building it.
  4. Compare and decide. Review response time and owner escalations against the baseline. Expand, adjust, or stop based on what changed.

The roadmap isn't "buy a customer portal." A portal might be useful later. First, find out whether the team can trust and maintain the information behind it.

5. Agree what is in scope, and what isn't.

A useful first engagement has a boundary. State which workflow you're examining, who needs to participate, what access is needed, and what the decision at the end will be. Separate discovery from implementation so neither side treats an untested idea as a build commitment.

At Rinsesoft, that can lead to discovery, systems architecture and implementation, or ongoing technology leadership, including fractional CTO support. Which one fits depends on the work. You don't need to choose the engagement before explaining the problem.

A one-page brief you can use today

Copy these prompts into a note before talking to your team or a technology partner. Short, concrete answers are enough. "We don't know yet" is useful too.

What happens now?
Describe one recent example, from the request to the finished work.
What would you like to have happen?
Describe the result for the customer, the team, or you as the owner.
What gets in the way?
List the waits, missing information, repeated work, and decisions that need you.
What must stay true?
Note budget, timing, security, existing tools, and business continuity constraints.
How would you know it helped?
Choose a measure, establish a baseline, and name who will review it.
What is the smallest useful first step?
Choose one change to test, what it depends on, and what you are not changing yet.

You bring the starting point and the destination.

You don't need all the answers between them. You need someone who can listen, make sense of the moving parts, and help you choose a practical path forward.
